\section{Procedure for approximating Error statistics on filter kernels}

One of the fundamental results in DSP is that the ideal window function
\[
w(t) = \mathrm{sinc}(t)
\]
in the time domain corresponds to a perfect rectangular pulse in the frequency domain~\cite{OppenheimDSP}. However, as the envelope $\tfrac{1}{x}$ decays the amplitude of $\sin(x)$ monotonically, the function has an infinite domain that is impossible to capture discretely. Thus, there is an associated truncation error $T(x)$ expressed as the error between the tail \& partial power series. This implies the existence of an associated error term $\mathbb{E}(x)$ that is at-least dependent upon the truncation point $N$.
\bigskip

\noindent The objective of this chapter is to do the following:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Establish a general method, unified under a bit allocation algorithm, to evaluate the absolute error $|\mathbb{E}|$ of the ideal LPF function $sinc(t)$ both in regards to compositions of the argument E.g. $sinc(t) \circ p(z), z \in \mathbb{C}$ \& convolutions (of course corresponding to multiplications in the frequency domain) of the form $sinc(t) \circledast p(z)$.
\item Derive a projector $R(x): \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}$ which dually embeds magnitude \& phase error in relation to the truncation point, $N$ \emph{WLOG} for parameters introduced in the following section.
\item Extend the idea of the projector to an orthonormal basis (namely, the \emph{Shannon Wavelett basis\cite{}}), introducing a general support kernel  $\widehat{H}(\omega)$. It is shown that all analytic functions are suitable candidates for 'sensitivity' based adjustments when expressed in terms of the aforementioned kernel in a manner befitting of FPGA's (\& hardware more generally.)
\item As a final theoretic step, illustrate the methods overall sensibleness (universal correctness not permitting), motivating the construction with some specific examples that show how compute can be saved.
\item Produce a hardware design that leverages properties of the construction to \textbf{(A)} Solve a bit allocation problem for a dyadic polynomial interpolating a non-arbitrary (sinusoidal) waveform \textbf{(B)} Apply a basic FIR, based on phase accumulation error inferred from the sensitivity matrix, to bandlimit the same signal with an edge guard \textbf{(C)} Make a dynamic adjustment to a coefficient search for a better interpolating polynomial by imposing a tighter bound (subject to magnitude sensitivity.)
\item Show results versus. established (or naive) methods.
\end{enumerate}

Further considerations this paper omits:

\begin{itemize}
\item In a local sense: regularizing the poor temporal, but excellent frequency, resolution (decaying $\propto \frac{1}{t}$)\cite{} of the Shannon Wavelett basis \& the excellent temporal, but poor frequency resolution of, for example, Haar functions.
\item Proving correctness to a standard incumbent of E.g. algorithmic proofs.
\item A priori selection of parameters (I.e. how one makes the choice for $N$)
\item Extending the idea of a single bandlimited example to bins, which naturally involves justifying the solution as a distribution. An intelligent parametric EQ design, for example, might need to infer how error correlates between bands (bins.)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Optimal Coefficient Quantization via Projection Methods}

Consider a function $f(x)$ represented as a polynomial series:
\begin{equation}
f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{N} c_n x^{2n}
\end{equation}
where $c_n$ are the series coefficients. As discussed, in fixed-point hardware implementations, these coefficients must be quantized to dyadic rationals of the form $\tilde{c}_n = a_n/2^{m_n}$, where $a_n \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $m_n$ determines the bit precision allocated to coefficient $n$.

The quantization error for coefficient $n$ is:
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_n = c_n - \tilde{c}_n
\end{equation}

The total approximation error introduced by coefficient quantization is:
\begin{equation}
E(x) = f(x) - \tilde{f}(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{N} \epsilon_n x^{2n}
\end{equation}

Here, our objective is to determine the optimal bit allocation vector $\mathbf{m} = [m_0, m_1, \ldots, m_N]$ that minimizes the projected error under a total bit budget constraint $\sum_{n=0}^{N} m_n \leq B_{\text{total}}$ for a LPF. Partially as a 'free bonus', but mainly by design, framing the problem in terms of an analytically continued series allows the projector $R(x)$ to expose a singular metric that packs in both 'phase' and 'bit' quantization error. This is effective on the computational front because hardware scheduling needs to reflexively adapt to error statistics in order to make intelligent inferences\cite{}. Finally, $sinc(x)$ is studied specifically for it's ability to be expressed within an orthonormal basis, the resulting hardware solution is then optimum in the sense that savings per atom are extensible to more complex waveforms. The approach is particularly well suited to subtractive synthesis, for example\cite{}.

Ultimately, the approach outlined here has several advantages which can't be  decoupled from any of the other contingent sections.\eqref{}

\subsection{Projection Operator and Error Metric}

Rather than minimizing the error in the standard $L^2$ norm, we introduce a projection operator $R(x)$ that captures the application-specific error weighting. The projected error is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}_{\text{proj}} = \langle E(x), R(x) \rangle = \int_{0}^{x_{\max}} E(x) \cdot \overline{R(x)} \, dx
\label{eq:projected_error}
\end{equation}
where $\overline{R(x)}$ denotes the complex conjugate of $R(x)$, and $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ represents the inner product in $L^2[0, x_{\max}]$.

Substituting the error polynomial:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}_{\text{proj}} = \int_{0}^{x_{\max}} \left[\sum_{n=0}^{N} \epsilon_n x^{2n}\right] \overline{R(x)} \, dx = \sum_{n=0}^{N} \epsilon_n \int_{0}^{x_{\max}} x^{2n} \overline{R(x)} \, dx
\end{equation}

\subsection{Sensitivity Matrix Construction}

The sensitivity of the projected error to coefficient $n$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
S_n = \left|\frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_{\text{proj}}}{\partial \epsilon_n}\right| = \left|\int_{0}^{x_{\max}} x^{2n} \overline{R(x)} \, dx\right|
\label{eq:sensitivity}
\end{equation}

This represents how strongly a unit error in coefficient $c_n$ affects the overall projected error. For a complex projector $R(x) = R_{\text{real}}(x) + iR_{\text{imag}}(x)$, we can decompose the sensitivity into real and imaginary components:
\begin{equation}
S_n^{\text{real}} = \left|\int_{0}^{x_{\max}} x^{2n} R_{\text{real}}(x) \, dx\right|, \quad
S_n^{\text{imag}} = \left|\int_{0}^{x_{\max}} x^{2n} R_{\text{imag}}(x) \, dx\right|
\end{equation}

The \textit{phase sensitivity} and \textit{magnitude sensitivity} can be defined as:
\begin{align}
S_n^{\text{phase}} &= S_n^{\text{imag}} \label{eq:phase_sens} \\
S_n^{\text{mag}} &= S_n^{\text{real}} \label{eq:mag_sens}
\end{align}

The complete sensitivity matrix is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix}
S_0^{\text{mag}} & S_0^{\text{phase}} \\
S_1^{\text{mag}} & S_1^{\text{phase}} \\
\vdots & \vdots \\
S_N^{\text{mag}} & S_N^{\text{phase}}
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Quantization Error Bounds}

For dyadic quantization with $m_n$ bits allocated to coefficient $n$, the quantization error is bounded by:
\begin{equation}
|\epsilon_n| \leq \frac{1}{2^{m_n+1}}
\label{eq:quant_bound}
\end{equation}

The worst-case projected error is therefore:
\begin{equation}
|\mathcal{E}_{\text{proj}}| \leq \sum_{n=0}^{N} S_n |\epsilon_n| \leq \sum_{n=0}^{N} \frac{S_n}{2^{m_n+1}}
\label{eq:error_bound}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Optimal Bit Allocation Strategy}

\subsubsection{Method 1: Sensitivity-Proportional Allocation}

Allocate bits proportionally to sensitivity:
\begin{equation}
m_n = m_{\text{base}} + \left\lfloor \alpha \cdot \frac{S_n}{S_{\max}} \right\rfloor
\label{eq:prop_alloc}
\end{equation}
where $S_{\max} = \max_n S_n$, $m_{\text{base}}$ is the minimum bit allocation, and $\alpha$ controls the dynamic range of bit allocation (typically $\alpha \in [3, 8]$).

The allocation is subject to the constraint:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{n=0}^{N} m_n = B_{\text{total}}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Method 2: Weighted Phase-Magnitude Allocation}

For applications requiring different emphasis on phase versus magnitude accuracy, define a weighted sensitivity:
\begin{equation}
S_n^{\text{weighted}} = \sqrt{w_{\text{mag}} (S_n^{\text{mag}})^2 + w_{\text{phase}} (S_n^{\text{phase}})^2}
\label{eq:weighted_sens}
\end{equation}
where $w_{\text{mag}}$ and $w_{\text{phase}}$ are application-dependent weights satisfying $w_{\text{mag}} + w_{\text{phase}} = 1$.

\subsubsection{Method 3: Greedy Optimization}

For the global optimum under constraint \eqref{eq:error_bound}, we employ a greedy algorithm. The algorithm proceeds as follows:

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Greedy Bit Allocation Algorithm:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Initialize $m_n = 1$ for all $n \in \{0, \ldots, N\}$
\item Set $B_{\text{remaining}} = B_{\text{total}} - (N+1)$
\item While $B_{\text{remaining}} > 0$:
    \begin{enumerate}
    \item Compute the error reduction for adding one bit to coefficient $n$:
    \begin{equation}
    \Delta_n = S_n \left(\frac{1}{2^{m_n+1}} - \frac{1}{2^{m_n+2}}\right) \quad \text{for all } n
    \end{equation}
    \item Find the coefficient with maximum error reduction: $n^* = \arg\max_n \Delta_n$
    \item Increment the bit allocation: $m_{n^*} \gets m_{n^*} + 1$
    \item Decrement the remaining budget: $B_{\text{remaining}} \gets B_{\text{remaining}} - 1$
    \end{enumerate}
\item Return the optimal bit allocation vector $\mathbf{m} = [m_0, m_1, \ldots, m_N]$
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent This greedy approach is optimal for the additive error model in \eqref{eq:error_bound} and has computational complexity $O(B_{\text{total}} \cdot N)$.

\subsection{Evaluation of Projected Error}

After bit allocation, the actual projected error can be computed as:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}_{\text{proj}} = \left|\sum_{n=0}^{N} (c_n - \tilde{c}_n) \int_{0}^{x_{\max}} x^{2n} \overline{R(x)} \, dx\right|.
\end{equation}

For numerical evaluation, discretize the integral:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}_{\text{proj}} \approx \left|\sum_{n=0}^{N} \epsilon_n \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} x_k^{2n} \overline{R(x_k)} \Delta x\right|,
\end{equation}
where $x_k = k \cdot x_{\max} / K$ and $\Delta x = x_{\max} / K$.

The real and imaginary components of the error are:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{E}_{\text{real}} &= \mathrm{Re}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N} \epsilon_n \int_{0}^{x_{\max}} x^{2n} \overline{R(x)} \, dx\right], \\
\mathcal{E}_{\text{imag}} &= \mathrm{Im}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N} \epsilon_n \int_{0}^{x_{\max}} x^{2n} \overline{R(x)} \, dx\right].
\end{align}

\subsection{Projector Derivation for Sinc Function}

The power series for $\mathrm{sinc}(x)$ is given as:
\[
\mathrm{sinc}(x) = \frac{\sin(x)}{x} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n}{\Gamma(2n+2)}x^{2n}.
\]

Due to the necessity of considering truncated power series, we introduce the upper bound $N$. Re-indexing over $n \in \{0,\dots,2N\}$ and using the Kronecker delta to select even terms:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sinc_proj}
\mathrm{sinc}_N(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{2N} \delta_{\,n \equiv 0 \pmod{2}} \cdot \frac{(-1)^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n+2)}x^{n}
= \sum_{n=0}^{2N} \delta_{\,n \equiv 0 \pmod{2}} \cdot \frac{i^n}{\Gamma(n+2)}x^{n},
\end{equation}
where the second equality holds because $i^n = (-1)^{n/2}$ when $n$ is even.

\subsubsection{Analytic Projector via Contour Integration}

The introduction of the Kronecker delta $\delta_{\,n \equiv 0 \pmod{2}}$ serves dual purposes: selecting even coefficients and facilitating analytic continuation. To make this precise, we let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{C}$ be a simply connected domain containing $[0, x_{\max}]$. Consider the space:
\[
\mathcal{A}(\Omega) = \{ f: \Omega \to \mathbb{C} \mid f \text{ is holomorphic on } \Omega \}.
\]

For $f \in \mathcal{A}(\Omega)$ with Taylor expansion about $z=0$:
\[
f(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a_k z^k, \quad a_k = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{\gamma} \frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta^{k+1}} \, d\zeta,
\]
where $\gamma$ is a simple closed contour in $\Omega$ enclosing the origin.

The projector $\mathbb{P}_N: \mathcal{A}(\Omega) \to \mathcal{P}_{2N}^{\text{even}}$ is defined via the modified Cauchy integral:
\[
\mathbb{P}_N[f](z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{\gamma} K_N(\zeta) \, f\!\left(\frac{z}{\zeta}\right) \frac{d\zeta}{\zeta}
\]
where $K_N(\zeta)$ is the \emph{selection kernel}:
\[
K_N(\zeta) = \sum_{n=0}^{N} \zeta^{2n} = \frac{1 - \zeta^{2N+2}}{1 - \zeta^2}.
\]

This kernel satisfies the projection property:
\[
\frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{|\zeta|=1} K_N(\zeta) \, \zeta^{-k-1} \, d\zeta = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } k \in \{0, 2, 4, \ldots, 2N\}, \\
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

\begin{lemma}[Parity selector via contour integral]\label{lem:kronecker-even-delta}
For $n\in\mathbb{N}_0$ and any $0<r<1$, define
\[
I_n \;=\; \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=r}\frac{dz}{z^{\,n+1}(1-z^2)}.
\]
Then
\[
I_n \;=\; \begin{cases}
1,& \text{if $n$ is even},\\[4pt]
0,& \text{if $n$ is odd}.
\end{cases}
\]
\begin{proof}
Let $h(z)=\dfrac{1}{1-z^2}$. For $|z|<1$,
\[
h(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty} z^{2m}.
\]
Writing $h(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a_k z^k$, we have $a_k=1$ if $k$ is even and $a_k=0$ if $k$ is odd. By Cauchy’s coefficient formula,
\[
I_n=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=r}\frac{h(z)}{z^{n+1}}\,dz=a_n,
\]
so $I_n=1$ when $n$ is even and $I_n=0$ when $n$ is odd.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Residue viewpoint.}
The integrand $\dfrac{1}{z^{n+1}(1-z^2)}$ has singularities at $z=0,\pm1$, but for $0<r<1$ only $z=0$ lies inside the contour. Hence the integral collapses to the single residue at $z=0$:
\[
I_n \;=\; \operatorname{Res}_{z=0}\!\left(\frac{1}{z^{n+1}(1-z^2)}\right).
\]
Expanding $\dfrac{1}{1-z^2}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}z^{2m}$ gives the Laurent series
\[
\frac{1}{z^{n+1}(1-z^2)}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty} z^{\,2m-n-1},
\]
so the residue (coefficient of $z^{-1}$) is $1$ iff $2m=n$, and $0$ otherwise. Equivalently, using $\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}\!\big(\frac{h(z)}{z^{n+1}}\big)=\frac{h^{(n)}(0)}{n!}$ for analytic $h$ with $h(z)=\frac{1}{1-z^2}$, we have $h^{(n)}(0)=n!$ for even $n$ and $0$ for odd $n$, yielding the same conclusion.
\end{proof}
\end{lemma}

Using the contour representation of the Kronecker delta (Lemma~\ref{lem:kronecker-even-delta}),
we can drop the Kronecker delta and express
\[
\mathbb{P}_N[\mathrm{sinc}](z\zeta)
= \sum_{n=0}^{2N} \left[\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\zeta| = r < 1} \frac{1}{\zeta^{\,n+1}(1 - \zeta^2)} \, d\zeta\right] \frac{i^n z^n}{\Gamma(n+2)}.
\]
Since the sum is finite, we may interchange the sum and the integral:
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb{P}_N[\mathrm{sinc}](z)
= \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\zeta| = r < 1} \frac{1}{1 - \zeta^2}
\left[\sum_{n=0}^{2N} \frac{(iz/\zeta)^n}{\Gamma(n+2)}\right] \frac{d\zeta}{\zeta} \equiv \mathbb{P}_N[f](z)
}
\]
This contour-based projector inherently filters even coefficients—the only non-vanishing terms in the $\mathrm{sinc}$ expansion.

\subsubsection{Evaluating the integral}

\[
\omega := \frac{i z}{\zeta},\qquad
S_{\infty}(\omega) := \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{\omega^{n}}{\Gamma(n+2)}.
\]

\[
S_{\infty}(\omega)
= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{\omega^{n}}{(n+1)!}
= \frac{1}{\omega}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\omega^{k}}{k!}
= \frac{e^{\omega}-1}{\omega},
\]
with the removable value \(S_{\infty}(0)=1\).
\medskip

\noindent \emph{Note:} The swap of sum and integral is still justified since $\left|\zeta\right| = r > 0 \implies \left|\omega\right| = \frac{\left|z\right|}{r}$ is bounded, so the exponential series is uniformly convergent on the contour.
\medskip

\[
S_{2N}(\omega) := \sum_{n=0}^{2N}\frac{\omega^{n}}{\Gamma(n+2)}
= \sum_{n=0}^{2N}\frac{\omega^{n}}{(n+1)!}
= \frac{1}{\omega}\sum_{k=1}^{2N+1}\frac{\omega^{k}}{k!}
= \frac{E_{2N+1}(\omega)-1}{\omega},
\]
where \(E_m(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}\) is the truncated exponential polynomial.

\[
\Gamma(m+1,z)=m!\,e^{-z}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}
\;\Longleftrightarrow\;
E_m(z)=\frac{e^{z}\,\Gamma(m+1,z)}{m!}.
\]
Hence, with \(m=2N+1\),
\[
S_{2N}(\omega)
= \frac{1}{\omega}\!\left(\frac{e^{\omega}\,\Gamma(2N+2,\omega)}{(2N+1)!}-1\right)
= \frac{e^{\omega}\,\Gamma(2N+2,\omega)}{\omega\,(2N+1)!}-\frac{1}{\omega}.
\]

\[
S_{\infty}(\omega)-S_{2N}(\omega)
= \frac{1}{\omega}\sum_{k=2N+2}^{\infty}\frac{\omega^{k}}{k!}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\bigl|S_{\infty}(\omega)-S_{2N}(\omega)\bigr|
\le \frac{e^{|\omega|}}{|\omega|}\,\frac{|\omega|^{\,2N+2}}{(2N+2)!}.
\]

Performing a change of variable $\frac{iz}{\zeta} \to \omega$ on the integral \& substituting:
\[
\mathbb{P}_N[\mathrm{sinc}](z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\omega| = |z|/r} \frac{1}{\omega^2 + z^2}
\left[\frac{e^\omega \Gamma(2N+2, \omega)}{(2N+1)!}- 1\right] d\omega
\]\eqref{}

\[
\mathbb{P}_N[\mathrm{sinc}](z)
= \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\omega|=\frac{|z|}{r}}
\frac{H(\omega)}{\omega^2+z^2}\,d\omega,
\qquad 0<r<1,
\]
with either choice
\[
H(\omega)=E_{2N+1}(\omega)-1
\quad\text{or}\quad
H(\omega)=\frac{e^{\omega}\,\Gamma(2N+2,\omega)}{(2N+1)!}-1,
\]
and \(E_{2N+1}(\omega)=\sum_{k=0}^{2N+1}\omega^k/k!\).

\[
\omega^2+z^2=(\omega-iz)(\omega+iz),\quad
\omega=\pm iz \text{ are simple poles inside the contour}.
\]
For any analytic \(H\),
\[
\operatorname{Res}_{\omega=\pm iz}\frac{H(\omega)}{\omega^2+z^2}
=\frac{H(\pm iz)}{(\omega^2+z^2)'}\bigg|_{\omega=\pm iz}
=\frac{H(\pm iz)}{2(\pm i z)}.
\]
\bigskip
\noindent \textbf{Note: }
\[
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_N[\mathrm{sinc}](z)
=\frac{e^{iz}-e^{-iz}}{2\,i\,z}
=\frac{\sin z}{z}.
\]
\emph{This is as expected. The phasor error contribution is solely contingent upon the effect of truncation (I.e. spectral bleed/leakage)}
\bigskip

\noindent Hence
\[
\mathbb{P}_N[\mathrm{sinc}](z)
=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint \frac{H(\omega)}{\omega^2+z^2}\,d\omega
=\frac{H(iz)-H(-iz)}{2\,i\,z}.
\]

\[
=\frac{E_{2N+1}(iz)-E_{2N+1}(-iz)}{2\,i\,z}
=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\frac{(-1)^m\,z^{2m}}{(2m+1)!}.
\]

\[
=\frac{e^{iz}\Gamma(2N+2,iz)-e^{-iz}\Gamma(2N+2,-iz)}{2\,i\,z\,(2N+1)!}.
\]

\[
\boxed{\;
=\frac{\Im\!\big(e^{iz}\Gamma(2N+2,iz)\big)}{z\,(2N+1)!}.
\;}
\]
This result is worth noting for the simplicity \& expectedness of its decomposition. We showed, via. standard complex analytic methods, that the partial sum $\mathrm{sinc}_N(z)$ can be rigorously expressed in it's incomplete upper gamma function form\cite{}. Moreover, that the projector error itself $\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{P}_N)$, depends precisely on the upper gamma function's bound $N_{\text{gamma}} \propto N_{\text{sinc}}, N_{\text{gamma}} = 2(N_{\text{sinc}}+1)$.
\bigskip

\begin{lemma}[For $x = z \in \mathbb{R}$, the truncation projector can be written in terms of a hypergeometric series]

\begin{proof}
Let
\[
\begin{aligned}
\theta &:= i z\\
S &:= 2(N+1)
\end{aligned}
\]

A holomorphic extension of the upper incomplete gamma function\cite{} given as:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\gamma(S, \theta) &= \theta^S \Gamma(S) \gamma^{*}(S, \theta)\\
\Gamma(S, \theta) &= \Gamma(S) - \gamma(S, \theta)\\
\gamma^{*}(S, \theta) :&= e^{-\theta} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{\theta^k}{\Gamma(S + k + 1)}
\end{aligned}
\]

can be applied to the expression $\Im(e^{\theta}\Gamma(S, \theta))$ as follows:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\Im(e^\theta \Gamma(S, \theta)) = \Im(\Gamma(S)e^\theta) - \Im\left(\Gamma(S)\theta^S \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{\theta^k}{\Gamma(S + k + 1)}\right)
\end{aligned}
\]

Clearly,
\[
\Im(\Gamma(S)e^\theta) = \Gamma(S)\sin(z)
\]
Since $S \in \mathbb{N}_0, S \geq 1 \implies \Gamma(S) = (S - 1)!$ and $z \in \mathbb{R}$

\[
\Gamma(S)\,\theta^S \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\theta^{k}}{\Gamma(S+k+1)}
= \theta^{S-1} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\theta^{k}\,\Gamma(S)}{\Gamma(S+k)}
= \theta^{S-1} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\theta^{k}}{(S)_k},
\]
\[
\text{where } (S)_k := \frac{\Gamma(S+k)}{\Gamma(S)} \text{ is the rising Pochhammer symbol.}
\]

\noindent The outside term $\theta^{S-1}$ will always be imaginary-valued, that is $\Re(\theta^{S-1}) = 0$ because $S - 1 = 2N + 1 \text{ is odd} \implies \theta^{S-1} \text{ is imaginary}$. We subsequently conclude, using the linearity of the $\Im$ operator over a series, that $\iff \text{k is even}$ will a term be included. Thus:

\[
\Im\!\left(\theta^{S-1} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\theta^{k}}{(S)_k}\right)
= -\,i\,\theta^{S-1}\,\Re\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\theta^{k}}{(S)_k}\right)
= -\,i\,\theta^{S-1}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\theta^{2k}}{(S)_{2k}}.
\]


$\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\theta^{2k}}{(S)_{2k}}$ can be represented in terms of the general hypergeometric series $\mathcal{H}_S(z)$ \cite{} as:
\[
\mathcal{H}_S(z) := {}_1F_2\!\left(1;\,\frac{S}{2}+1,\;\frac{S}{2}+\frac{3}{2};\,-\frac{z^2}{4}\right).
\]

\[
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\theta^{2k}}{(S)_{2k}}
= -\,\frac{z^{2}}{S(S+1)}\,\mathcal{H}_S(z).
\]

\[
-\,i\,\theta^{S-1}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\theta^{2k}}{(S)_{2k}}
= \frac{i\,\theta^{S-1}\,z^{2}}{S(S+1)}\,\mathcal{H}_S(z)
\equiv \frac{(-1)^{N+1}\,z^{S+1}}{S(S+1)}\,\mathcal{H}_S(z).
\]

\[
\boxed{
\implies \Im\!\big(e^{\theta}\Gamma(2N+2,iz)\big) = \Gamma(z)\sin(z) - \frac{(-1)^{N+1}\,z^{S+1}}{S(S+1)}\,\mathcal{H}_S(z)
}
\] as desired.
\end{proof}
\end{lemma}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}_N[\mathrm{sinc}](z) &= \frac{1}{\Gamma(S)z} \left[\Gamma(S)\sin(z) - \frac{i\theta^{S-1}z^2}{S(S+1)} \mathcal{H}_S(z) \right]\\
&= \boxed{
\mathrm{sinc}(z) + \frac{(-1)^N z^S}{\Gamma(S+2)} \mathcal{H}_S(z)
}
\end{aligned}
\]

\section{Extending definition of the projector to accomodate generic $H(\omega)$}

\noindent Although deriving the statement using complex analysis may seem contrived it is motivated by four (4) concrete principles:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The fact that any analytic $H(\omega)$ can be evaluated in a systemic fashion (I.e. in terms of it's residue.)
\item The fact that both the relative phase and quantization error (for sufficiently large $N$) can be dually evaluated
\item We may pick the $H(\omega)$ kernel in such a way that error is minimized relative to each term (that is, on a per-term basis) in the power series of $\mathrm{sinc}(z)$ itself. This is of the form $H(\omega) \circ p(z)$.
\item We may also pick the $H(\omega)$ kernel in such a way that error is minimized relative to the application. This is of the form $H(\omega) \circledast p(z)$
\end{enumerate}

\newcommand{\PP}{\mathbb{P}}
\newcommand{\odd}{\mathrm{odd}}
\newcommand{\even}{\mathrm{even}}

\paragraph{Setup.}
Given a \emph{mask} $S\subset\mathbb{N}_0$ on the even indices, define the 0/1 selector
\[
s_n:=\begin{cases}1,& n\in S,\\ 0,& n\notin S,\end{cases}
\qquad
K_S(\zeta):=\sum_{n\ge 0} s_n\,\zeta^{2n}.
\]

\paragraph{Fourier-side representation (masked wave–forming).}
Assume $f$ admits the Fourier representation
\[
f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat f(\omega)\,e^{i\omega z}\,d\omega,
\qquad \widehat f\in L^1(\mathbb{R})\cap L^2(\mathbb{R})\ (\text{e.g.\ compactly supported bandlimited}).
\]
Then, by termwise integration (Tonelli/Fubini under the stated integrability),
\[
\boxed{\quad
\PP_S[f](z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat f(\omega)\,
\Phi_S\!\big((i\omega z)^2\big)\,d\omega,
\qquad
\Phi_S(t):=\sum_{n\ge 0}s_n\,\frac{t^{\,n}}{(2n)!}\quad}
\]
i.e.\ the projector replaces each plane wave $e^{i\omega z}$ by the \emph{even masked kernel} $\Phi_S((i\omega z)^2)$.

\paragraph{Example: $\PP_S$ acting on $\operatorname{sinc}_\pi$ and frequency “edge–shaping”.}
Let $\operatorname{sinc}_\pi(z):=\frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\pi z}$ with Fourier transform
\[
\widehat{\operatorname{sinc}_\pi}(\omega)=\mathbf{1}_{[-\pi,\pi]}(\omega)
\quad\text{(Shannon’s bandlimited model \cite{Shannon1949}).}
\]
Applying $\PP_S$ yields
\[
\PP_S[\operatorname{sinc}_\pi](z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\Phi_S\!\big((i\omega z)^2\big)\,d\omega
=\sum_{n\ge 0} s_n\,\frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)!}\,(\pi z)^{2n}.
\]
Two masks illustrate “wave–forming” while remaining a projector ($s_n\in\{0,1\}$):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Truncation to degree $\le 2N$:} $s_n=\mathbf{1}_{\{0\le n\le N\}}$ reproduces the classical even Taylor projection.
\item \emph{Edge–shaping mask:} $s_n=1$ for $n\in\{0,M\}$ and $0$ otherwise. Then
$\Phi_S((i\omega z)^2)=1+(\omega z)^{2M}/(2M)!$, increasing weight near $|\omega|=\pi$ in the band integral; in DSP terms this emphasizes band edges while preserving linear phase (cf.\ window/sidelobe tradeoffs \cite{Harris1978}).
\end{itemize}
